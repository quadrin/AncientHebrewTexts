Copper Scroll Places

Which named places in the Copper Scroll can scholars identify on a map, and how well does each identification hold up against the text? This page scores every published proposal for the 61 entries and maps the ones that survive, at the level of the site. It also tests every published explanation of the scroll's seven groups of Greek letters.

Site-level identifications only. These are not deposit locations. The maps show where scholars place the scroll's named places and landmarks, such as Kh. Qumran or the Kidron at Mar Saba. The scroll's cubits, depths and directions were not converted into positions, and nothing here proposes or supports excavation.

How the candidates were scored

Every place that any edition or study proposes for an entry was listed, including proposals that fail. The sources are the editions of Milik (1960, 1962), Lefkovits (2000) and Puech (2006, 2015), the essays in Copper Scroll Studies (2002), Høgenhaven (2020), the Survey of Western Palestine (1881–83) and Elitzur (2004). Each candidate was then tested four ways:

  1. Name test. Elitzur's two conditions for an "almost positive" identification: an ancient source fixes the area, and a modern Arabic name matches in all or almost all letters, at or near that area. A name that only resembles counts as weak evidence.
  2. Feature fit. Does the published description of the site include the landmark the text requires: a cistern, a tomb, an aqueduct, a spring?
  3. Sequence fit. Does the site sit in the same area as the neighbouring entries? Sequence alone never makes a site best-supported.
  4. Reading dependence. A name that exists in only one edition's reading caps the verdict at "possible", unless the other evidence is strong.

No theory of who hid the treasure was allowed to choose between readings. Each candidate ends with one of four verdicts and a confidence level:

  • medium best-supported, medium confidence
  • low best-supported, low confidence
  • possible possible
  • weak and ruled out: kept in the tables, not mapped

No identification reached high confidence, before or after the archaeology check. Of the 292 candidates, 23 are best-supported, 88 possible, 141 weak and 40 ruled out.

Contested cases

Where scholars disagree, each position is set out with its evidence. evidence marks what a source says; inference marks a judgement, with its confidence.

The Valley of Achor עמק עכור · entries 1, 17

evidence
Milik and Puech follow the late tradition of the Onomasticon, "north of Jericho" and "beside Galgala", and place the scroll's Achor in Wadi Nuweiʿimeh. Allegro and Eshel prefer el-Buqeiʿah, the usual identification of the biblical valley. TIR prints "Achor Vallis" north-west of Jericho.

inference · medium–high
On the 1880 survey sheet the wadi rises at the springs of ʿAin Duk north-west of Jericho and runs east past Kh. el-Mafjar. TIR's north-west and Milik's north-east are two stretches of one wadi, not rival sites. The choice between this late Achor and the Buqeia is still open.

Koḥlit כחלת · entries 4, 11, 15, 16, 19, 60

evidence
Twelve proposals are on record, from Jericho to Mount Carmel. No ancient text outside the scroll places Koḥlit.

inference
Unknown. Tell es-Sultan (Puech, Høgenhaven) is the leading working hypothesis, rated possible, low. Carmel, Transjordan and the Samarian desert break the sequence of the entries.

Sekakah and Qumran סככא · entries 20–23

evidence
The placement rests on V 13, "from Jericho to Sokokah", and on the water installations at Qumran. The aqueduct (21) is the firmest single landmark. Eshel's reading of a "dam" at 20 is rejected by Puech and doubted by Høgenhaven.

inference · medium
Best-supported at site level, but it fails the name test: no name survives at Qumran.

Doq דוק · entry 31

evidence
1 Macc 16:15 fixes Dok by Jericho, and the spring ʿAin Duk keeps the name. Milik and Eshel place the entry at the Hasmonean fortress on the summit of Jebel Qarantal; the 1883 Survey preferred Kh. Abu Lahm. Puech reads the landmark as a drying floor, Milik as a fortress. Puech's 2002 essay says "north-east" of Jericho; his 2006 and 2015 texts say north-west.

inference · medium
The only full name-test pass near Jericho. The name belongs to the spring, so the summit placement is still an inference.

Absalom's monument יד אבשלום · entry 48

evidence
Puech and Høgenhaven place it in the Kidron, beside the neighbouring Kidron entries. Milik put it in the south-west necropolis, through the chain King's Valley = ha-Šoʾ = Bethso = Baqʿah, which Puech does not fully accept.

inference · low
Kidron best-supported, south-west weak. The King's Valley is also placed south of the city for entries 36–37, so one valley currently sits in two places. This is an open question.

Bethesda בית האשוחין · entry 55

evidence
The name "Bethesda" exists only in Milik's emended reading. Puech reads "the house of the two reservoirs" and calls the location "not completely certain". The twin pools are a known first-century installation (John 5:2; Eusebius).

inference · medium
The double pool fits both readings, so the verdict stays best-supported even though the name depends on an emendation.

Masada (44), Kephar Nebo (42), Qobʿeh (41) and Baal-Hazor (33) exist only in Milik's emended text, or in a reading that Puech shows came from a misplaced fragment. They are rated weak or ruled out.

What the archaeology says

For each best-supported place, and for the main "possible" ones, published excavation and survey reports were read for one question: is the kind of landmark the entry names (an aqueduct, a pool, a tomb, a cistern) reported at the site, and was it in use in the late Second Temple period? A match of type and period makes a site compatible with an entry; it does not identify the scroll's own feature.

  • Four landmark types match for the period, and all are water installations: the Qumran aqueduct (21), the Wadi el-Qelt channels at Choziba (32), the Bethesda twin pools (55) and the springs of Beth Shean (58). Tombs, cairns, pits and dovecotes never match a specific entry. Water works survive and are dated more often than those, so this also reflects what archaeology can see.
  • Siloam (49) stays medium, and only if the scroll really names Siloam. That reading needs an engraving error corrected and a word restored; Lefkovits reads no place name there. The pools and the tunnel outlet fit the setting, but the trough the entry names is not established. Two pools compete for the name: Reich and Shukron identify their large stepped pool at Birkat el-Ḥamra as Siloam, while Szanton (ʿAtiqot 113, 2023) identifies it as Solomon's Pool and the smaller Silwan pool at the tunnel outlet as Siloam.
  • Ramat Raḥel is lowered to low. Its known pool enclosure lies under a fill with pottery as late as the second century BCE, and in the first century the site was a small village. That weakens the match to the known pools; it does not prove that no reservoir existed.
  • Absalom's monument stays low. The standing Kidron monument is first-century by style, but its earliest inscriptions name Zacharias, not Absalom.
  • Doq stays medium. A Hasmonean fortress stood on the summit, which suits Milik's reading of the landmark word; the archaeology is not allowed to decide between readings.
  • Seven placements had no known landmark. For none of them does a published report describe the entry's own landmark at the site in the period.

Revised on 28 September 2026 after a review of the Phase 5 evidence, which withdrew a first upgrade of Siloam to high. Reports that could not be read (Magen on Mount Gerizim, Zertal's survey for Kh. Ibziq, Patrich on Mar Saba and Hyrcania) could still change individual checks.

What stays open

  • 37 entries have possible places only. Most fall in areas that the sequence implies: the Temple area (3, 5, 7, 8, 12, 12a, 13, 14), the Jericho oasis (27, 29, 30, 33) and the Tekoa–Herodium sector (39–45). For these the honest result is that no specific site can be identified.
  • Entry 41 has no place name in the reading accepted here, so there is nothing to map.
  • The King's Valley. One valley for entries 36–37 and 48, or two?
  • ʿAṣla (18). Milik's middle stretch of one torrent, or the "little Wady el ʿAsala" that Clermont-Ganneau crossed near the coast?
  • Doq (31). Summit fortress, spring, or Kh. Abu Lahm?
  • The landmarks. For the seven placements with unknown feature fit, does any published report describe the required landmark at the site in the first century? This is the next phase of the work.

The Greek letters

Seven short groups of Greek letters are engraved in the first four columns of the scroll. Every edition and study in the files was read for the readings and for the explanations proposed, and each explanation was tested against the text. Any claim that matches letters to names or words was compared with what chance alone would produce.

What the tests show

  • Where the letters stand is not chance. All seven close an entry, and all seven are in columns I–IV. Either pattern would arise by chance about 3 times in 10,000. The letters stop at IV 2, although five more entries follow on the same sheet.
  • They are not numerals for the amounts. Read as Greek numerals, no group equals its entry's amount (ΚΕΝ = 75 after "seventeen", ΘΕ = 14 after "sixty-five"). Only ΣΚ is even a well-formed numeral. The one numeral fit that has been published needs at least four different rules for five groups.
  • Name matches carry little weight. Six of the seven groups begin at least one proper name in Josephus. But a random, name-like two-letter group does so 87% of the time. Only ΚΕΝ, as in Kenedaios (War 2.520), adds real weight.
  • "Chageiras" is a restored name. At War 5.474 the printed Greek reads "and agiras, which means 'lame'". The reading ΧΑΓ = Chageiras depends on restoring the name there.
  • No link to place or treasure. Marked and unmarked entries are spread alike over the Jericho and Jerusalem placements and over the kinds of treasure.
  • Still unknown: what the letters mean, and why they stop at IV 2. Photographs of three lines and 3D scans of the metal would settle the disputed readings and whether the letters were added later.
