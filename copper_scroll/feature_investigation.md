## Evidence discipline

The atlas now separates three questions:

- **Reading:** does the wording needed for this identification survive, or depend on restoration, emendation or a disputed interpretation? “Mixed” means the central words survive but some spatial constraints are restored. “Disputed” means alternative readings change the locality or installation.
- **Site:** how well does the proposed locality satisfy the reading? Existing medium/low judgments are retained. These are qualitative comparisons, not calibrated probabilities.
- **Exact feature:** has the particular installation been distinguished from alternatives at that site? All five remain unresolved. This does not mean that every ancient installation there is unknown.

Review the text before using archaeology to select a reading. A repeated identification, an archaeological type match and a dated, uniquely matching installation are different kinds of evidence. Missing evidence is inconclusive where the relevant publication is inaccessible or the remains were destroyed.

The machine-readable counterpart is [`atlas/app/atlas-evidence.json`](https://github.com/quadrin/AncientHebrewTexts/blob/main/copper_scroll/atlas/app/atlas-evidence.json). It supplies the atlas’s feature comparisons and source-access notes. The companion CSV records spatial constraints and rejection tests for candidate sites and individual features.

## Entry 21: Sekakah and Qumran

### Textual constraints

Puech 2006, p. 188, and Lefkovits 2000, p. 185, were checked against page images. These are editions, not independent observations of the original metal.

The head of a water conduit and Sekakah are the stable basis for comparison. The word connecting them is damaged: “west of,” “leading to” and “at” have different spatial implications. The noun “stone” is restored. A conspicuous present-day boulder cannot validate the restoration that was used to select it. The northern relation also needs its grammatical attachment fixed before a geometric test is possible.

Milik 1960, p. 147, identifies the conduit with Qumran’s aqueduct and cites de Vaux 1956, p. 538. His identification depends on the geographical argument about Sekakah on p. 146. Puech’s proposed restoration itself invokes Qumran; that reasoning must not be treated as an independent proof of the location.

### Archaeological alternatives

The useful comparison is among the **intake basin**, the **upper-channel junction**, and the **tunnel mouth**. The first is the best functional starting hypothesis; the latter two test whether “head” could refer to a different division of the system. This is an investigative ordering, not a confidence ranking of identified hiding places.

[Stacey 2009, “The Dam”](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/stacey), reports the loss of the dam and infers it from the basin and aqueduct. He also proposes different phases for run-off collection and the main aqueduct. His chronology remains a hypothesis to check against stratigraphy. His cited plan is Ilan and Amit 2002, pp. 380–386, Fig. 1; the original plan was not obtained in this review.

The atlas’s ground photograph is a cistern outlet. It is context for the water system, not evidence locating the head of the conduit.

### Independence audit

The six Phase 5 records are Eshel, Milik 1960, Milik 1962, Wexler, Puech and Høgenhaven. Milik’s two records repeat a proposal; Puech’s restoration and Høgenhaven’s agreement do not add separate excavation observations. Eshel’s survey synthesis points back to Masterman, de Vaux, and Ilan–Amit. Wexler supplies a broad system summary. This establishes a source trail, not six independent confirmations.

### What would change the assessment

1. An independent reading that excludes the spatial relation required by a candidate would weaken that candidate.
2. A plan showing the proposed feature is a downstream outlet rather than a head would weaken the intake claim for that feature.
3. Construction dating wholly later than the relevant Second Temple context would weaken that installation, while leaving the possibility of an earlier system open.
4. A source-based plan with defensible control points, an appropriate construction phase and a match to the independently selected wording could justify a feature polygon. The current gazetteer anchor cannot.

Do not turn the proposed dam’s approximate location into a measured surviving footprint. Do not treat the survival of a stone as evidence for a deposit.

## Entry 32: Koziba and Wadi Qelt

Puech 2006, p. 192, and Lefkovits 2000, p. 236, were visually checked. The editions disagree over Koziba/Buz and the second landmark’s wording or meaning. Keep spring/outlet and retaining-wall/mountain interpretations separate.

The [Survey of Western Palestine III, pp. 227–228](https://archive.org/stream/surveyofwesternp03conduoft/surveyofwesternp03conduoft_djvu.txt), was checked in its digitized transcription. It distinguishes the spring channel, the division at Jisr ed-Deir and a cliffside wall opposite Deir el-Kelt carrying channels at different levels. Its proposed Roman or Byzantine dating is provisional. Its observations support comparison among features; they do not identify the scroll’s outlet.

The three comparisons are therefore:

1. **ʿAin Qelt and a channel head:** test a spring or source interpretation.
2. **Jisr ed-Deir:** test an outlet or distribution-junction interpretation.
3. **The wall opposite Deir el-Kelt:** test whether the second landmark and the outlet are demonstrably connected in the relevant period.

Eshel’s period aqueduct synthesis is available here through Phase 5 extraction. Milik’s claim that later works preserve an earlier plan does not date every visible wall. A modern monastery pin cannot stand for all three features.

Obtain the original route and construction-phase plans before ranking these more finely. Record repaired masonry separately from any earlier channel bed. Reject a particular pairing if its outlet and wall cannot have functioned together under the independently chosen reading. If the word means mountain, the retaining-wall test ceases to discriminate.

## Entry 49: Siloam

The reading remains conditional; this review makes no fresh palaeographic judgment on X 15–16. The restored name and disputed letter require separate specialist review.

[Szanton, ʿAtiqot 113 (2023), pp. 29–44](https://publications.iaa.org.il/atiqot/vol113/iss1/3/) was read in full via [an accessible copy](https://jamestabor.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Szanton-Antiquot-2023-Siloam-and-Solomons-Pools.pdf). The printed year is 2023; the portal recommends 2024. Page images checked: Fig. 1, p. 34; Fig. 4, p. 37; Fig. 6, p. 41. These distinguish the outlet pool from Birkat el-Ḥamra and show the original plan/sections and outlet stones. Fig. 6 identifies railing stones, not the scroll’s trough. The article discusses both pool identifications and uneven dating evidence; it does not establish a Copper Scroll feature.

Keep the smaller Silwan outlet pool and the large stepped pool separate in the candidate comparison. The currently displayed ground image shows the latter. First settle the required words, then ask whether a dated trough or gutter is attested at either location. The existence of pools, steps and drainage is insufficient by itself. Do not use entry 22’s Solomon’s Pool in Sekakah to silently transfer that entry to Jerusalem.

## Entry 31: Doq

A summit fortress fits one reading; its existence cannot decide which word was engraved. Regional cave occupation in the ten source records does not attest the required eastern corner.

The first decisive deliverable is an independent letter-by-letter review of VII 11. If it supports a guardhouse, obtain a dated summit plan and identify the eastern corner. If it supports a drying place, search published installation inventories for evidence of that function. Failure to find such an installation is weaker evidence where later construction erased earlier structures.
