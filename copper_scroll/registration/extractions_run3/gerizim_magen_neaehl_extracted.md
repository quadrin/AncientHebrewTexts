## 3. Mount Gerizim Excavations III: The Coins (JSP 19, 2021)

Page concordance. Section One PDF (j.ctv2bwvt5v.5): printed page = PDF page − 1. Section Two PDF (j.ctv2bwvt5v.6): printed page = PDF page + 77. Printed pages are given below.

### 3a. Date and nature of the destruction

- **Evidence, p. 42.** "Mt. Gerizim yielded hundreds of coins dated after 128 BCE; the last coins from the site are dated to 112–110 BCE, and one coin to 110/109 BCE."
- **Evidence, p. 42.** "It is therefore more likely that Mt. Gerizim was destroyed in 111–110 BCE than in 129 BCE … We do not discern that the city at Mt. Gerizim was conquered in two phases."
- **Evidence, p. 104 (Bijovsky).** "the conquest of Mt. Gerizim by John Hyrcanus I did not take place before 110/109 BCE."
- **Evidence, p. 39.** "The city at Mt. Gerizim was destroyed in a conflagration that left its mark everywhere." "The burning of the city implies that there was no intent to resettle it". "The fire was started after the booty and the bodies of the dead had been collected."
- **Evidence, p. 41.** Siege phase: "Streets were blocked … entrances and windows were sealed". "The southeastern citadel of the sacred compound did not have its own independent water supply, and the dozens of jars discovered on the pavement of the central hall apparently provided the defenders with water."

### 3b. Occupation after the destruction: a Hasmonean garrison

This is the only post-110 BCE activity on the summit that the volume puts forward.

- **Evidence, pp. 39–40.** "Until the time of Alexander Jannaeus, and possibly even later, a Hasmonean garrison remained in the city to prevent the Samaritans from returning to Mt. Gerizim."
- **Evidence, p. 105 (Bijovsky).** "A disproportionately large number of 528 coins of this king [Jannaeus] were discovered at Mt. Gerizim. There is no doubt that most coins reached the site after it was destroyed by fire by John Hyrcanus I." Magen's view is cited: "a Hasmonean garrison was built at the site to preclude the return of the Samaritan population, and the presence of the garrison explains the large quantities of Jannaeus coins".
- **Evidence, p. 105.** 490 of the 528 Jannaeus coins (93%) are the crude "anchor within circle / star within circle" type. "There is a wide consensus that this was Jannaeus' last type, struck after 80/79 BCE". Some may have been "struck by one of his successors during the reign of his widow Salome."
- **Evidence, p. 104.** Hasmonean coin ratio: John Hyrcanus I 68, Aristobulus I 3, Jannaeus 528.
- **Evidence, p. 129 (Bijovsky summary).** The Jannaeus coins "apparently reached the site after its destruction. They seem to be related to the Hasmonean garrison built there to avoid the return of the Samaritan population."
- **Evidence, p. 39 (Magen), a different explanation in the same volume.** The many Jannaeus coins "are related to the flourishing of the Temple in Jerusalem, the large-scale construction of palaces and fortresses, and the many wars", i.e. pay for mercenaries and builders. This is general, not specific to the site.
- **Evidence, p. 46.** On Ant. 14:100: the Romans "besieged … at Mt. Gerizim" were "Possibly … located in abovementioned fortress built by the Hasmoneans that guarded the road to the mount, on the northern slope of Mt. Gerizim that overlooks Neapolis."
  - **Inference.** The book calls this Alexander "Alexander Jannaeus, son of Aristobulus". Ant. 14:100 concerns Alexander son of Aristobulus II (57/55 BCE), not Jannaeus. The slip does not change the point about the fortress. It places a Hasmonean-built fortress on the **northern slope**, not on the summit.
- **Inference.** The volume gives **no find-spots** (area or locus) for the Jannaeus coins in the passages read. So it cannot show whether the garrison used any staircase or the courtyard cistern. It shows only that someone on the mountain was handling late-Jannaeus-type coins, i.e. into the 70s BCE or later.

### 3c. From Pompey to 70 CE: abandonment, with stray coins only

- **Evidence, p. 59.** "the latest coins found at Mt. Gerizim related to the destruction of the city and the temple are the coins of Alexander Jannaeus, indicating the end of Samaritan settlement on Mt. Gerizim until modern times. After being destroyed, the site was abandoned for around 400 years".
- **Evidence, p. 59.** The only coins from 63 BCE to the end of the 1st c. CE: Augustus as, Rome, 7 BCE; Ambibulus, 9/10 CE; three of Festus, 58/59 CE; Nero, Caesarea, 68 CE; an autonomous coin of the 1st c. CE (Ascalon, p. 106). "No coins have been found from the two Samaritan attempts to ascend and take control of Mt. Gerizim, in the days of Pontius Pilate, 26–32 CE, and Vespasian, in 67 CE. The seven coins mentioned above are not related to historic events occurring at the site, but came there by chance."
- **Evidence, p. 106 (Bijovsky).** Cat. Nos. 411 (Augustus), 412 (Ambibulus), 413 (Festus), 414 (Ascalon), 415 (Nero). No find-spots are given in the discussion. (The catalogue, pp. 130–204, was not searched for loci.)
- **Evidence, p. 46.** "The excavations at Mt. Gerizim reveal that the Hellenistic city was not restored or rebuilt after its destruction."
- **Evidence, p. 46.** The Samaritans "were not part of the Roman reconstruction program" of Pompey and Gabinius and "were not permitted to rebuild their temple and national center".
- **Evidence, p. 48.** "Pilate's vigorous and violent response left no doubt that the Roman objective was to prevent the Samaritans' return to the mount."

### 3d. Roman and Byzantine reuse of the summit

- **Evidence, p. 46.** "A Roman temple to Zeus Hypsistos was erected on the northern summit of Mt. Gerizim in the mid-second century CE." "In the third to fourth centuries CE the Samaritans restored the sacred precinct". "toward the end of the fourth century, a Roman public building was built: a fortress typical of the many fortresses built by Theodosius I". "The sacred precinct was seized in 484 CE by Emperor Zeno, who built a Church to Mary Theotokos at the site."
- **Evidence, p. 59.** Coins: 2nd c. CE, 3 Hadrian, 2 Antoninus Pius, 1 Faustina II. Then one Severus Alexander. The sequence is "renewed from the second half of the third century CE and continued until the Islamic period."
- **Evidence, p. 59.** Area S, L844: a silver hoard of 44 coins, Aurelian to Diocletian.
- **Evidence, p. 60.** The date of the late-3rd/early-4th-c. coins "is similar to that of the building found next to the sacred precinct in the north of the mountain, close to the stairs that ascend to Mt. Gerizim". Doubts remain "whether it is a pagan building or a Samaritan synagogue".
  - **Inference.** "the stairs that ascend to Mt. Gerizim" here are on the northern (Neapolis) side. In the passages read, this is the only link between a staircase and post-Hellenistic activity. The volume does not say which staircase this is, or whether it is one of the Hellenistic ones in the question.
- **Evidence, p. 117 (Bijovsky).** A hoard was found "in the long-ruined Hellenistic citadel (Area S), built projecting some 4 m beyond the line of the southeastern corner of the sacred precinct's eastern wall. The citadel was destroyed toward the end of the Hellenistic period, but was apparently still accessible at the end of third century CE".

## Summary for Q47 (inference, from JSP 19 only)

- The coin evidence dates the destruction to **111–110 BCE or a little after**.
- It supports a **Hasmonean military presence** on or near the mountain into the reign of Jannaeus (**late-type coins, post-80/79 BCE**, 490 of them). The volume places a Hasmonean fortress on the **northern slope** road.
- From Pompey to 70 CE the volume reports **no occupation**, only seven stray coins it calls chance finds. It says the city was never rebuilt and the site was "abandoned for around 400 years".
- Nothing in JSP 19 shows the Hellenistic staircases or the courtyard cistern **in use or repaired** in the 1st c. BCE–70 CE. It also does not rule out a garrison using them, because it gives no find-spots for the garrison coins.
- The answer for the specific structures depends on NEAEHL (§1) and JSP 8 (§2), which are still unread.
