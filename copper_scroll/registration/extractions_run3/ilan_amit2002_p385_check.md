## What the volume says about the Qumran aqueduct's date (from other pages)

- **Evidence (snippet), p. 18 (editors' introduction).** "The Qumran aqueduct is probably Herodian as well." The context lists Herodian systems: "the Naḥal Qidron aqueduct to Hyrcania, and the systems of Masada and Herodion".
- **Evidence (snippet), p. 38 (introductory chapter on literary sources).** The Copper Scroll: "The only one whose identification is likely is 'the aqueduct (*amat mayyim*) in the valley of Sekaka' (5.1) (fig. 1), apparently the site of Qumran, where indeed a rainwater collection system exists, and may date back to the Hellenistic period (see Ilan and Amit, below pp. 380-86)."
- **Evidence (snippet), p. 39.** "If the composition is to be classified as fiction, as Milik believed, there is no point in applying any geographical data that may be a loan from the Scripture – such as the toponym Sekaka – to actual topographical elements identifiable on the ground."
- **Evidence (snippet), p. 20.** "Desert systems terminate at water cisterns cut on the slope around the summit of the fortress, or, in the case of Qumran and Jericho palaces, at the settlement itself. The water cisterns of the fortresses had rock-cut ceilings, while those of Qumran were like open pools, presumably protected by a covering cloth, or by a second storey."
- **Evidence (snippet), p. 37.** The only Archelaus aqueduct the volume mentions is at Archelais: "the aqueduct branch built by Herod's son Archelaus to the village he founded near Jericho, Archelais". Josephus (AntJ 17.13.1) is cited as a terminus ad quem for the aqueduct from 'Ein el-'Auja to Na'aran.
- **Inference.** Within this one volume the editors give two different dates for the Qumran system. p. 18 says "probably Herodian". p. 38 says "may date back to the Hellenistic period" and cites Ilan & Amit for it. Neither passage mentions Archelaus for Qumran.
